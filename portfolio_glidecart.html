Brand Identity & Naming
Naming the Concept and Building Its Visual System

The Concept

Glidecart started as a question: what would it feel like to shop with a cart that already knew where it was going? The name had to say two things at once — quiet, effortless motion ("glide") and the familiar object it was replacing ("cart"). The visual system built on that tension: a rounded, minimal wordmark paired with a cool navy-and-cyan palette meant to read as calm technology, not sci-fi spectacle.

In My Own Words

"I wanted the brand to feel closer to a well-designed appliance than a robot. Cyan against deep navy reads as precise and quiet — the same register as the product experience I was trying to design: helpful, not intrusive."

Product Experience Concept
Designing the In-Store Experience

The Concept

The hardest part of designing Glidecart's experience wasn't the cart — it was the interface moment when a shopper decides whether to trust it. The onboarding screen had to explain "follow mode" and "list mode" in under five seconds, and the in-aisle display had to stay legible and calm even in a bright, busy store.

In My Own Words

"Every screen went through the same test: could my mother understand it in one glance? If not, it got simpler. Trust is the whole product here — the interface's job is to earn it quietly."

Pitch & Presentation Design
Building the Case for the Concept

The Concept

An invention concept needs a document that can stand on its own in front of someone who has never heard of it. The pitch deck lays out the problem, the product experience, and a plain-language explanation of the sensor-guidance approach — built to be read by a non-technical audience without losing the substance.

In My Own Words

"I write and design for non-technical audiences constantly in my compliance work — this deck applies that same discipline: real substance, explained so anyone can follow it."
