Brand Identity & Naming
Naming the Concept and Building Its Visual System

The Concept

GlideCart started as a question: what would it feel like to shop with a cart that already knew where it was going? The name says two things at once — quiet, effortless motion ("glide") and the familiar object it's replacing ("cart"). The mark pairs a rounded cart glyph with a navy-to-cyan gradient, meant to read as calm technology, not sci-fi spectacle — closer to a well-designed appliance than a robot.

In My Own Words

"Cyan against deep navy reads as precise and quiet — the same register as the product experience I was trying to design: helpful, not intrusive."

The Technical Vision
What It Would Actually Take to Build This

The Concept

Near-term, GlideCart's navigation and item-recognition problem is a classical one: computer vision to identify products, LIDAR or similar sensors to map the store and avoid obstacles, and a routing algorithm to find the shortest path through a shopping list. Longer-term, the concept explores where quantum computing could eventually add value — optimization algorithms (like QAOA) for routing at scale, and quantum-safe encryption for payment security. Neither is deployable today; both are legitimate open research questions worth tracking.

In My Own Words

"I wanted to think through the real engineering stack, not just wave at 'AI and quantum' as buzzwords — even in a concept exercise, the technical honesty matters to me."

Honest Framing

Current quantum processors are noisy and limited to roughly 50–100 usable qubits — nowhere near enough for a production routing or encryption workload. Any real GlideCart build today would run on classical AI and sensor navigation (the same category as Amazon's or Instacart's existing smart-cart pilots), with quantum computing treated as a multi-year research horizon, not a current feature.

Product Experience Concept
Designing the In-Store Experience

The Concept

The hardest part of designing GlideCart's experience wasn't the cart — it was the interface moment when a shopper decides whether to trust it. The onboarding screen had to explain "follow mode" and "list mode" in under five seconds, and the in-aisle display had to stay legible and calm even in a bright, busy store.

In My Own Words

"Every screen went through the same test: could my mother understand it in one glance? If not, it got simpler. Trust is the whole product here."

Pitch & Communication Practice
Translating One Idea for Different Audiences

The Concept

Beyond the visual identity, this exercise included drafting the kind of communication package a real concept needs: a plain-language executive summary, a short promotional-video script, and a how-to walkthrough of the technical approach. The point wasn't to produce a fundraising deck — it was practice at explaining the same idea at three different altitudes, from a 30-second pitch to a technical walkthrough.

In My Own Words

"I write and design for non-technical audiences constantly in my compliance work — this was the same discipline applied to a product idea instead of a policy document."
