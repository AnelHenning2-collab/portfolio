Compliance, Explained

NIST CSF Without the Jargon

The five-function framework behind most security programs, translated for the people who aren't on the security team but are still part of it.

AH
Anel Henning · Writing sample · 7 min read

Here's a scenario: your department gets a new laptop-tracking spreadsheet, sits through a phishing test, and fills out an incident report template — all in the same quarter, none of it explained as connected. It is connected. All three are part of the same framework, and once you can see the shape of it, every one of those "random" security asks starts making sense.

That framework is the NIST Cybersecurity Framework, and despite the name, you don't need to be technical to understand it. It organizes everything a security program does into five plain functions.

The Five Functions, in Plain Language

🔍
Identify
Know what devices, data, and systems you have.
🛡️
Protect
Put safeguards in place before something goes wrong.
👀
Detect
Notice when something unusual is happening.
🚨
Respond
Take action right away when a problem is found.
🔄
Recover
Get back to normal and learn from what happened.

None of these require a security certification to understand, because none of them are exclusively the security team's job. That laptop-tracking spreadsheet from the opening example? That's Identify — you can't protect what you don't know you have. The phishing test is testing Detect, at the individual level. The incident report template exists for Respond.

Where This Gets Real

A scenario worth sitting with

An employee reports their laptop was stolen from their car. Reporting it immediately — before anything else happens — is the Respond function. Getting a replacement device set up and data restored afterward is Recover. Notice that both matter, and neither works without the other: responding fast without a recovery plan just means a fast, disorganized scramble.

This is the part most jargon-heavy explanations skip: the framework isn't a checklist owned by IT. It's a shared vocabulary for who does what, when. Once a non-technical team understands that a shared drive lock is Protect and a strange login alert is Detect, security stops feeling like something that happens to them and starts feeling like something they're already part of.

Why This Matters for How Compliance Content Gets Written

Most NIST CSF explainers are written for people who already work in security, which means they use security vocabulary to explain security vocabulary. The framework doesn't need that. It needs the same treatment you'd give any five-step process a new hire has to learn — plain nouns, real scenarios, and a reason to care that has nothing to do with fear of an audit.
